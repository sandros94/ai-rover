# Decisions

Current, settled design decisions with their reasons. This file describes the present; history lives in git. Open questions are at the end.

## Product

- **Fully autonomous rover.** Users interact only by submitting a destination for the next segment and approving others' submissions with an LGTM (no dislikes). One live submission per user. The author of the segment in progress may submit, but everyone else's submission takes precedence: theirs ranks last whatever its LGTMs, and wins only when nobody else submitted during the whole segment.
- **Submitting is an LGTM on your own entry.** A new submission starts with its author's LGTM; people may LGTM as many entries as they like.
- **Real-time pacing.** A segment takes its real duration at real rover speed (100 m ≈ 40–60 min). The drive window is the submission-and-voting window, which is the project's natural rate limit. Replays scrub at 60–200×. Reason: the only mission is exploration, and this cadence suits a casual visitor checking in a few times a day.
- **Perseverance is the reference** for mass (1,025 kg), size (3.0 × 2.7 × 2.2 m), wheels (6 × 52.5 cm, rocker-bogie), speed cap (152 m/h) and operational tilt limit (16°, up to 20°+ as risky). See `research/rover-physics-terrain.md`.
- **Mars, throughout.** Gravity 3.71 m/s², the Mars sol, and Mars-like terrain (regolith plains, craters, ridges, dunes) so every Perseverance figure stays truthful with a single set of references. A lunar mission with its own rover is a possible later addition, not a variant of this one.

## Submissions and the map

- **A submission is a point.** The user clicks any spot on the 2D map inside the survey circle, fogged ground included; the server snaps it to the nearest vertex (the nearest pathable one when the ground there is seen). No typed text: the vote card shows the submitter (name and avatar, via a foreign key to the user) and computed facts only: distance, estimated time, max slope, hazards, Jev confidences.
- **Browser and server plan with the same code.** The browser rebuilds the server's stop disk exactly from the served chunks and mask (reachability and viewshed included) and runs the shared `planGoal` (snap, time band, death zones, plan, path clearance) in a Web Worker; a test holds the preview polyline byte-equal to the server's. Route colours follow seen slope (green under 6°, amber under 12°, red above) and dash where unseen.
- **Map interaction.** North up; one finger or the mouse pans, pinch or wheel zooms, a tap is a pick, and nothing submits without the confirm button.
- **Goals may lie in the fog.** A seen goal must be traversable and reachable over believed ground (seen vertices by their real traversability, unseen ones counted passable), so `unpathable` never reflects a barrier the fog hides; an unseen goal is taken at the vertex picked without reading its height, and the drive discovers the truth. Beyond the survey circle answers `outside`. Reasons are decided from seen vertices and the circle alone; the browser preview refuses identically.
- **Judged attempts are capped per round.** A user may create at most `maxJudgedPerRound` (default 5) submissions in a round, whatever their later status; rule refusals are never stored and never count. The cap is checked before any planning or Jev call.
- **A submission binds to the round it was planned against.** The insert locks the round and compares stop and anchor with what the plan used; a mismatch answers `round-changed` and the client re-plans.
- **Preview is client-side, judgment is server-side.** While the user hovers or drags, the browser runs the same deterministic planner over the same terrain data and draws the path and slope profile. Jev is called only when a submission is actually made, and the server re-plans authoritatively on submit; the client preview is never trusted. This bounds Jev spend to real submissions.
- **The map is fogged by what the rover has seen.** At each stationary point the server computes a binary viewshed by line of sight from mast height (about 2 m) over the heightmap, on CPU, and accumulates it over the journey, so ridges and crater rims hide what lies behind them. The picker shows the revealed terrain's relief in 2D. A scouting drone that reveals terrain ahead is a planned later feature.
- **The planner knows only what the rover has seen.** A segment is planned on revealed terrain, with unseen cells carrying a penalty cost as in Perseverance's AutoNav. The producer then drives the real terrain and discovers hidden ground mid-drive.
- **Intermediate checkpoints.** When hidden ground turns out blocked or unsafe (a cliff edge, a blocked pass), the rover stops at the last safe pose and that stop becomes a checkpoint in its own right rather than a failure: the segment's progress is kept, the journal records the shortfall, and new submissions start from there. Failure is reserved for hazard events.
- **Each stationary point has a manifest.** A JSON manifest (validated with valibot, version 2) carries an integer `version` for future migrations, the world config hash, the world geometry the client needs (chunk size, cell size, mast height, slope limit), the chunk coordinates and blob keys of the disk, and the key of the accumulated revealed mask, stored as its own sparse binary blob per stop while chunks stay immutable and cacheable forever. Blob keys are pure functions: `terrain/{hash}/chunks/{cx}_{cy}.bin`, `missions/{missionId}/revealed/{stop}.bin`, `missions/{missionId}/stops/{stop}.json`.
- **Terrain is computed on demand, server-side, and served as data.** The height function is deterministic from the mission seed, but the expensive parts (chunks, cost map, pathability, viewshed) are computed only for the survey around each newly reached stationary point, a circle of the mission's stop radius (500 m by default) plus a margin. The resulting chunks and revealed mask are stored as blobs and served through the CDN; browsers fetch them and never generate terrain themselves, so everyone sees the same ground and the client cannot manipulate it. CPU time, cost and speed of this step are benchmarked and optimised over time.

## Navigation

- **Route by Theta\*, execute as arcs.** The server plans a segment with any-angle Theta\* over the 1 m cost map (slope² cost, impassable above the slope limit, unrevealed cells at a penalty as in AutoNav), then smooths the polyline into turn-in-place and constant-curvature arc motions for execution, each driven from rest to rest on a jerk-limited profile. Plain A\* is the fallback if server compute proves too heavy in testing.
- **Navigation metrics are first-class and see only revealed ground.** Every plan reports path length, straight-line distance, detour ratio, slope statistics, unrevealed fraction, turn count, expansions and compute time. Slope statistics are computed over revealed vertices only, because the metrics feed Jev's state and the public telemetry, and either would otherwise leak hidden terrain.
- **Biomes are planned.** Terrain generation will carry distinct biomes (plains, crater fields, dune fields, rocky ground and so on), and the rover "analyses" the ground it sees so that people can read those findings on the map and plan differently for each. Not designed yet; recorded so the terrain model keeps room for it.

## Architecture

- **Code plans and simulates; Jev judges.** Jev returns typed judgments (Choice / Noul / Score) over state that code supplies and is documented as unreliable at arithmetic, so it never produces driving commands. Code runs the path planner and the producer; one Jev request per submission returns `feasible`, `distance_band`, `distance_confidence`, `time_confidence`, `risk` and `exploration_value`, the last judged from the exploration parts and the recent stops with discovery stated as the mission's purpose. The confidence scores are the vote tie-break weights. The confidence scores are the vote tie-break weights; the verdict bands are reject below 0.2 feasibility, review to 0.8, accept above. Results are cached by a hash of model, state and questions, and tests run on recorded fixtures. See `research/typesafe-jev.md`.
- **Keyframes are the source of truth.** Every attempt (successful or failed) is one immutable record: plan, keyframes at a fixed rate, events (stop, replan, slip, failure), outcome. Clients interpolate; they never re-simulate. Replay determinism therefore survives engine changes.
- **Keyframes are binary at 2 Hz.** Twenty-three float32 per frame (time, position, quaternion, speed, six wheel spins, two rocker and two bogie angles, four corner steering angles), 662 KB per hour of driving before deflate, with events, reveal deltas and outcome alongside. Interpolation between frames is a shared, tested function: position on a cubic Hermite whose tangents are the recorded speed along the recorded heading, heading and steering angles on monotone cubics, the quaternion slerped for pitch and roll, and spins, height and suspension advancing with the distance covered, so velocity, heading rate and steering rates carry across frames without a kink and the wheels roll as far as the rover moves. It needs no field beyond the frame's own speed and heading. At 2 Hz the interpolated wheels sit within 0.03 mm of the re-solved pose, a thousand times inside the 3 cm bound. The layout is versioned: keyframe and slice format v2 carry the 23 values and segment manifest version 2 states stride 23; readers still take v1 (19 values, published before steering was recorded) and read its wheels as straight, since published blobs are immutable.
- **Rover pose is a six-unknown point-contact solve.** Body height, pitch, roll, differential and both bogie angles are solved by Newton iteration so that every wheel centre sits exactly one radius above the terrain at its actual position; the ACE closed-form equations seed the solve. The returned angles and quaternion rebuild the wheel positions, which the tests check. Consequence for rendering: frames carry the full pose, and interpolating them at 2 Hz keeps the wheels within 0.03 mm of the ground, so the renderer uses interpolated frames as they are and never re-solves; only a coarser frame rate would need re-solving.
- **Ground contact is verified numerically, not visually.** Tests sample interpolated poses between keyframes and check every wheel contact point against the analytic terrain height within a tolerance, and check suspension angles against the ground under each wheel, so a rover floating above or sinking into rough ground is caught without a screenshot. Browser screenshots remain a secondary check.
- **Kinematic producer.** Pose from heightmap samples and surface normals along the planned path, speed limited by slope and roughness, slip as a deterministic function of slope, clearance checked at rover scale. This mirrors how JPL's own planner checks safety (its clearance evaluator is kinematic). Rigid-body simulation is deferred unless the kinematic model proves inadequate.
- **No push transport.** Netlify hosts no server-side WebSockets and streams for at most 60 s. Live state therefore comes from precomputed keyframes served through Blobs and the CDN, played back locally against wall-clock, plus a small CDN-cached mission-state endpoint polled every few seconds. A split Cloudflare worker for WebSocket fan-out may be studied later. See `research/platform-nuxt5-netlify.md` §4.
- **Persistence.** Netlify Database (Postgres) holds live relational state: users, submissions, likes, rounds, segments and stops. Access goes through Drizzle ORM (1.0 release candidate) over the platform connection's pool with the driver matching the connection (Neon's WebSocket pool inside Functions, node-postgres elsewhere); the platform's HTTP client is never used, because Drizzle's combined driver calls it in a form the current client refuses, so plain queries failed while transactions worked; the TypeScript schema is the contract and generated SQL migrations live in `netlify/database/migrations/` where the platform applies them at deploy.
- **Relational invariants the schema enforces.** Eight tables (`user_account`, `user_identity`, `mission`, `stop`, `round`, `submission`, `submission_like`, `segment`), UUIDv7 ids made in code, snake_case columns. At most one open round per mission and one open submission per user per round (partial unique indexes); a closed round has a winner and no open submissions; likes change only on open submissions; a segment is created as `driving` with its private outcome and settled to `arrived`, `stopped-short` or `failed` only after its end time, so status, death positions and the reached stop never leak early; `attempt` counts retries from the same stop. Check constraints guard every status set and the settled-segment shape.
- **During a drive, submissions are anchored on the planned destination.** The next round opens as the drive starts, anchored on the winner's goal (public knowledge) and planned over the from-stop's disk with the revealed mask as it was when the drive began, so nothing the drive discovers leaks through plans, refusal reasons or metrics. At settlement: arrived → submissions stand; stopped short → each open submission is re-planned from the actual stop, invalid ones rejected with a reason and valid ones re-judged; failed → open submissions are marked lost and a fresh round opens from the retry stop.
- **A new stop's blobs are published only at settlement.** Until the last slice is released the stop, its revealed mask and its manifest exist nowhere a client can fetch.
- **Blob keys:** chunks are keyed by world hash (identical across missions on the same world); stops and revealed masks are keyed per mission.
- **The tick holds its lock only to write rows.** Everything slow runs before the mission lock: planning, judging, simulating the winner and publishing the blobs (immutable and keyed, so a blob nobody names yet is harmless); inside the lock the tick checks the mission is still where the preparation found it, applies rows, and otherwise writes nothing and leaves the mission due for the next tick. Open rows are locked and applied, withdrawn ones skipped, goals submitted meanwhile rejected as invalidated; candidates that cannot be driven are rejected, and with none left the round is voided and reopened from the same stop. The transaction sets lock, statement and idle-in-transaction timeouts so a killed function cannot hold the lock. A public read never waits for a tick: it serves the current state and, when the mission is due, dispatches the tick to a platform background function (`netlify/functions/mission-tick-background`, 15-minute limit, invoked on the request's own origin with a token derived from the session key, at most once per mission per 30 s per instance) with a try-lock; writes tick inline and answer 503 with a retry hint when the lock times out. The background entry is bundled by the platform, not Nitro, so it imports only Nitro-free modules and CI proves the built bundle loads.
- **Mission state is cheap to read.** `mission.next_due_at` records the earlier of the drive's end and the round's close; reads tick only once it has passed and take no lock otherwise. `GET /api/mission` is public and CDN-cached (5 s, stale-while-revalidate 30 s, durable, tagged per mission); writes tick, stay `no-store` and purge the tag. The client polls with `no-cache` and corrects its clock by the `Age` header.
- **Jev answers persist.** The judgment cache is a database table keyed by the request hash, write-once, so cold starts and parallel instances never re-bill or re-draw. Upstream failures answer a fixed message; the cause is logged.
- **The vote closes when the drive ends.** The round for the next segment is open while the current drive plays. When the last slice is released the round closes and the most-liked submission wins. If no submission exists at that moment the rover idles; the first submission then opens a 5-minute **planning phase** so others can compete before it wins.
- **Likes, with diminishing returns.** One like per user per submission, retractable while the round is open, self-like allowed. The winner ranks by √likes × (1 + exploration): each extra like counts less than the previous one, so a following buys an edge, never a lock, and a destination in unexplored ground counts for up to twice its likes. Ties break by exploration, then Jev's risk score (lower first), then the sum of the distance and time confidence weights, then earliest submission; the weights and tie-break are mission configuration.
- **Exploration is measured from public data.** Each submission carries three parts in 0..1: the share of its path over fog, whether its goal is in fog, and a pocket bonus when the goal's fog is enclosed by seen ground or lies near a driven path (leftover fog the rover passed by). Their weighted mean is averaged with Jev's own exploration score; the value sits on the vote card with the parts on hover. It reads the revealed mask and past paths only, never heights.
- **Risk is public.** The risk score sits on the vote card next to distance, time, slopes and the confidence weights, so voters see what the tie-break sees.
- **Failure zones are tunable constants.** A new submission may not end within 30 m of a death position and its planned path may not pass within 15 m of one. Three failures whose death positions lie within 50 m of each other reset the rover to the previous checkpoint. All three radii live in mission configuration for later analysis and tuning. Netlify Blobs hold the immutable journal, one blob per attempt, served through a function with durable CDN caching and cache tags.
- **Auth.** JWE cookie session via `unauth` (h3 v2) + `unjwt`, with a nuxt-auth-utils-like DX (`defineOAuth…EventHandler`, `useUserSession`) implemented on that toolset. GitHub and Discord OAuth hand-rolled on one authorization-code handler (state and, for Discord, the PKCE verifier sealed in the flow cookie; the redirect URI is the request's own URL; GitHub asks no scope, Discord only `identify`; a denied authorization fails as refused). GitHub names the user by its login, Discord by `global_name`, else the username, and shows the CDN avatar or Discord's default one; the settings page labels each linked platform by its handle. AT Protocol OAuth hand-rolled as a _public_ client (`token_endpoint_auth_method: none`), login-only: PAR + PKCE + DPoP proofs signed with `unjwt` (ES256), randomness and hashing from `unsecure`, handle → DID → PDS → authorization-server discovery in project code, encrypted state cookie, no tokens stored. Zero extra dependencies.
- **One account, several identities, at most one per provider.** Each `user_identity` keeps the profile its provider gave at the last sign-in with it. `user_account.primary_provider` names the identity whose name, avatar and handle the account shows (copied onto the account whenever it changes); it starts as the identity that created the account, the repository keeps it pointing at one of the account's own identities, and the settings page changes it (`PATCH /api/_auth/account`), re-issuing the session with the new name. A development login has no identity and keeps its own name until one is linked.
- **Linking merges.** A signed-in user starts any provider's flow with `?link`; the flow cookie records who, and the callback holds only while that user is still signed in. An unclaimed identity joins the account. An identity held by another account brings that account over in one transaction: its identities, submissions (with the drives they won) and pauses move, its LGTMs and not-moving flags move with a shared one collapsing to a single row (the later flag stands), an open submission of the old account in a round where the current one has its own is withdrawn as its author would, and the old account row is deleted. Submissions per round are counted, never stored, so the cap on them applies to the merged total from then on. Sessions still naming the old account answer `USER_GONE` and sign out, as for any deleted account. A merge where both accounts hold an identity of the same provider is refused; the user unlinks one first. A failed link lands on the settings page with its reason. Unlinking (`DELETE /api/_auth/identities/{provider}`) refuses the last identity, the only way back in, and hands the primary role to the oldest remaining one.
- **Public profiles and a private settings page.** `/u/{id}` (`GET /api/users/{id}`) shows what vote cards and the journey already made public: name, avatar, the providers linked (as icons), member since, and every submission that stood in a round (open, won or lost; withdrawn and rejected ones never), with its round, goal distance, LGTMs, status and, once the drive to it settled, how it ended and how far it went; the tally (submissions, rounds won, metres driven on their goals, LGTMs from others, deaths on their goals) derives from that list. `/settings` is for the signed-in user only and is built of sections, "Linked platforms" first. The header's avatar opens a menu with Profile, Settings and Sign out.
- **Stack.** Nuxt 5 nightly (`nuxt-nightly@5x`, Nitro 3, h3 v2), Nuxt UI 4, valibot, TypeSafe JS SDK pinned to `jev-1.13.0` against the public API with the project's own key, or through the hosting platform's AI gateway when no key is set and the platform injects one (Netlify's gateway serves Jev and bills credits); the base URL is always explicit, since the SDK would otherwise take a gateway URL from the environment while the project's key is meant for the public API. Jev responses are cached by hash of state + questions + model and recorded as test fixtures.
- **The local database is brought up to the deploy's migrations at boot, by the platform's own applier.** A development-only Nuxt module (bundled into no production or test build) runs `applyMigrations` from `@netlify/database-dev` against the emulated database when the dev server starts, so the local schema is the deploy's equal with the same tracking table, never a second hand-maintained one. It touches only a loopback database, records the digest of every applied migration file, and refuses to serve (503 on every request, with the reset remedy) when an applied migration was edited or removed. A Nuxt DevTools "Database" tab shows status, applies migrations, resets the database in place, seeds a mission and clears the local journey store. Tests apply migrations the same way.
- **Shared modules are import sources only.** Nitro's bundled auto-import scanner misses `class X extends Y` declarations and would inject an error class into the file that declares it, breaking the production build; the shared modules import each other explicitly, so they are excluded from injection in `nitro.imports.exclude`. The newer Nitro beta removes `imports` from its config type while still honouring it at runtime, so the template's pin stays until Nuxt's nightly and Nitro agree.
- **Netlify dev libraries are patched locally where needed.** `@netlify/nuxt` targets h3 v1 and `@netlify/database-dev` pins an older PGlite major while Netlify Database runs PostgreSQL 18; both are kept working through pnpm overrides or patches in this repo rather than by dropping emulation or downgrading Nuxt.
- **A segment is computed in full when it starts, and released over time.** The first request after a vote closes plans and simulates the winner end to end inside a synchronous function, before the database lock: route, every metre of driving over the true terrain, discoveries, slip, reveals, the safe stop or the failure. The server knows the outcome immediately; clients do not. The record is stored as 30 s time-indexed slices, and a slice is served only once wall-clock has passed the end of its window, so nothing in the future is ever served; the live view trails the simulation by at most one slice. The public segment manifest carries neither outcome nor duration; the outcome rides in the final slice. The tick that plans and simulates runs in the platform's background function, outside any request's time limit.
- **Failures come from hidden ground and slip.** The plan avoids known hazards, so the producer fails a segment when unrevealed terrain turns out impassable within one step, or when a deterministic slip model (slip grows with slope and with a per-cell loose-regolith property of the terrain, seeded like everything else) accumulates past a stuck threshold. Loose regolith is the first ground property the rover "analyses" and the seed of the biome idea.
- **Speed follows the references, without per-metre stops.** Perseverance thinks while driving, so the effective AutoNav rate (0.033 m/s on flat ground, falling to half at the slope limit; the 0.042 m/s physical cap is only the "commanded" reference) is the driving speed itself. The rover stops only for stated reasons: steering, turning in place at up to about 1.5°/s (the outer corner wheels at the 0.042 m/s wheel-speed cap), a 20 s assessment before every replan, and a 30 s imaging stop every 25 m, skipped in the first and last 5 % of the path as planned (the planner's time estimate applies the same rules); both stop lengths are judgement calls with no published figure. Events are `start`, `steering`, `turning`, `assessing`, `replan`, `imaging`, `slip`, `blocked`, `hazard`, `stuck`, `arrived`; the rover's status at any time derives from them, and the HUD shows it so a stop never looks like a fault. The simulation steps at 2 Hz (about 2 cm per step) with the path probed every 25 cm ahead; 10 Hz gave the same outcome to 1e-12 m at four times the cost.
- **Motion accelerates like a 1,025 kg rover.** Every motion is jerk-limited on one seven-segment profile (a triangle when a move is too short to reach its top rate): the wheels ramp from rest to the cruise speed and back to rest before every stop (steering, turning, imaging, assessing, a stop short), and between cruise speeds as the slope changes, the cruise speed commanded once per step; a turn in place and each steering ramp their angular rates the same way, and the recorded speed and wheel spins follow the profile. Drive acceleration 0.025 m/s² and jerk 0.04 m/s³ are judgement calls, since published figures on the rover's acceleration are scarce: rest to 0.033 m/s takes about 2 s over about 3 cm, visible at 1×. The turn's angular limits are the drive's at the outer corner wheels, and the steering joints' the drive wheels' (the actuators are identical), so a turn ramps in about 2.3 s and steering in about 2.4 s. A stop comes to rest first and happens where the rover rests, about 3 cm on; a fault under the rover (a failing pose, getting stuck) ends the drive at a 0.1 m/s² emergency deceleration, also a judgement call. Outcomes, slip and the stuck rule are unchanged. The planner's estimate adds each rest-to-rest run's ramp time and the profiled turns and steering through the same helper. See `research/rover-physics-terrain.md` §8.
- **The four corner wheels steer, standing still, before the motion that needs it.** As on Curiosity and Perseverance, the middle wheels are fixed and the rover steers, then drives. Each motion has one wheel stance about its turn centre (double Ackermann): a turn in place points every corner wheel's axle at the body centre, about 48° at the front and 45° at the rear, the two sides rolling opposite ways; an arc points each wheel along the tangent of its own circle, the inner wheels harder; a straight keeps all four at 0. Every wheel rolls in proportion to its distance from the centre, which sets the six recorded spins. Before a motion whose angles differ from the current ones by more than 2°, the rover stands and steers all four at once on one profile topping at 0.168 rad/s (about 7.4 s into or out of a turn-in-place stance), a `steering` stop the HUD shows as "steering wheels"; so a turn in place and each blend arc are steered into and out of. Arcs stay at or above the 1.17 m radius the ±85° steering limit allows; the 2 m blend radius sits above it. The planner's estimate counts the same steering, and stays within 10 % of the drive (the recorded fixture drive, 6 % short; a 100 m slalom with seven turns in place, 1 % short; 150 m on seed mars, 6 % long). See `research/rover-physics-terrain.md` §7.
- **Unrevealed ground costs a flat penalty in planning, whatever its true slope.** Otherwise the planner would read terrain the rover has never seen and hidden hazards could never be discovered mid-drive. Replans trigger only for newly seen blocking ground within 10 m ahead, capped at 20 per segment.
- **A stop's reachability is seeded from the nearest traversable vertex to its centre, not the centre itself.** The rover reached the stop, so the ground there is reachable by definition; seeding from an untraversable centre vertex would leave every goal unpathable and the mission stuck. Nearest means Euclidean, ties to the lowest grid index; the viewshed still starts from the centre, where the rover stands.
- **Reveals are computed with the drive and delivered with it.** A short-range viewshed every metre and the full viewshed at the stop are part of the segment record; the client's map grows as the time-gated slices arrive.
- **A segment is bounded by planned time, 15 minutes to 2 hours,** not by distance: the planner's estimate uses the producer's speed and stop models, with fog at the unrevealed penalty's speed, and sits within about 10% of the real drive. A distance ring made newcomers' goals unelectable; time is what the vote cadence needs. Tunable.
- **Route-choice experiment is offline.** A script over recorded terrain fixtures has code generate 3–8 routes and Jev pick one, compared against the planner's own cost ranking; the result decides whether route selection ships.

## Client

- **Live playback trails wall-clock by one slice plus a few seconds.** A slice is released only after its window ends, so the live view runs one slice behind, with a small margin to fetch it; sim time never passes released slices, and replay rejoins live when it catches up. The outcome is shown only when playback reaches it, never when the last slice merely loads.
- **The browser reads through one data layer.** Composables fetch stop manifests, chunks (progressively: pick ring and viewport first), revealed masks and segment slices (gated by release time), decode the binary formats, run the shared interpolation and keep a playback clock synced to wall-clock; every visual reads from it, and it is tested on recorded fixtures without a browser.
- **3D rendering conventions.** Z up throughout, chunk and overlay buffers relative to their own origin for float32 precision, two-level LOD (full resolution within 150 m of the rover, one vertex in four beyond, with skirts and hysteresis), pixel ratio capped by the quality tier (below), routes as thin ribbons rather than GL lines. The terrain is lit: a Lambert material over vertex colours carrying the ground state's tint, with normals from the true slopes at the 2D hillshade's 2.5× exaggeration, turned up to the vertical as fog thickens so light never shows the relief fog hides; fogged ground is mixed to the fog colour after lighting, per vertex. The sun is a directional light with PCF shadows (map size by tier, blur radius widening as the sun lowers) over a square following the camera target, 30 m in half side and growing in steps with camera distance up to 120 m, shrinking back only 10 % inside a step; chunk skirts cast none, so seams draw no lines. The shadow map is framed so it redraws texel for texel while nothing it shows moved: its centre snaps to whole texels across the light and whole metres along it, and the light follows the sun only in 0.1° turns (about every 25 s of sol at real pace), since each turn re-rasterises every edge. The rover's shadow is cast by its low-poly model (drawn with neither colour nor depth in the view), the full model casting none: at 3–12 cm texels the shadow shows none of the full model's detail, which cost three quarters of the shadow pass. A hemisphere light carries the sky's fill and the ground's bounce, and a sky dome draws horizon, zenith, the glow around the sun and its disc. Lit materials go through AgX with a look on three's custom tone mapping hook (no post-processing pass); unlit overlays (route, driven path, death discs) are not tone mapped and keep their token colours. Exposure is analytic, from the level-ground irradiance of the sun and sky, and eases to its target with a 1.5 s time constant. The rover model draws with its own physically based materials baked per moving part into atlas pages, 30 draw calls, and 25 more for its low-poly shadow caster (its low-poly stand-in one flat lit material, shared); its metals and glass reflect an environment prefiltered from the sky's colours at the skylight's intensity, which adds reflections only (the hemisphere light stays the one diffuse sky light, for the rover as for the ground) and is repainted only when the sky's colours change. At night its only light is the arm turret's white LEDs around the WATSON camera (the real rover carries no headlights): once the sun's centre sets, the arm unstows into a night pose that holds the turret about 1.9 m up over the front deck, WATSON's boresight on the ground 3 m ahead of the front wheels, and once it rises stows by the same path played backwards. The path is six straight legs between keyframes in joint space (lift: the elbow opens and the wrist folds with the shoulder still, lifting turret and wrist straight off their rests; clear: the elbow opens further and the wrist unfolds part way, carrying the wrist 3 cm clear before the shoulder moves, since turning the shoulder earlier swings it back towards its rest; turn: the shoulder turns and the elbow opens while wrist and turret tilt the turret up and the forearm off the deck; swing: the shoulder turns and raises into its night angles while the elbow opens most of the way, which keeps the turret off the mast; elbow; aim: wrist and turret), each timed by its furthest-moving joint (the others in proportion, so a leg's joints start and stop together) along a jerk-limited S-curve from rest to rest, peaking at 2°/s reached in 1.5 s (2°/s², 4°/s³), 164 s of the sol in all: design choices, the real unstow being a sequence of minutes whose joint rates and accelerations are not published. Every scripted mechanism motion runs on the same S-curve helper (`motionProfile`), so each accelerates and decelerates like a mass; a move too short to reach its peak rate peaks lower with no cruise. The shoulder cannot swing with the elbow still folded: the turret would sweep through the mast. A function of the clock alone, so replays scrub to any point of it. The night pose is a design choice for the view, not a configuration the rover drives in; the model's tests hold it within the URDF's limits and at least 5 cm clear of the mast, the deck and the wheels over the suspension's travel, and the whole timed path, every 0.1 s (at most 0.2° of any joint), at least 3 cm clear once each link has left its rest (a link's gap only grows while it lifts off), all measured on 5 cm boxes of the model's own geometry. The lamp hangs from the turret along the boresight, a 24° cone of 15 cd reaching 9 m.
- **The stop scene draws on demand, at a quality tier per viewer.** A frame is drawn only when something in it changed (a prop, the camera, a model arriving, the exposure settling) and then at most `frameCap` times a second; orbiting, zooming, the pointer over the scene and a focus easing in draw at the display's rate, for a second after the last input. Nothing in the scene moves faster than a few centimetres a second, so at a stop the scene draws nothing and while driving it draws at the cap whatever the display's refresh rate. Tiers `high | medium | low` set explicit knobs (`shared/utils/client/scene/quality.ts`): shadows (ground and rover, rover only, or off), shadow map size, the range around the camera target within which ground casts shadows (farther ground only receives), the pixel ratio cap, the frame cap, and the camera distance past which the rover draws its low-poly model, in the stand-in look, in place of the full one (back only 10 % nearer). High: full shadows within 120 m, 2048² map, pixel ratio up to 2, 120 fps, low-poly rover past 60 m. Medium: full within 64 m, 2048², 1.5, 60 fps, 40 m. Low: rover shadow only, 1024², 1, 60 fps, 20 m. The default tier comes from the device (low for two cores or 2 GB; phones medium only with six cores and 4 GB; other touch devices medium; desktops high from eight threads and 8 GB, unreported memory counting as enough), the viewer's choice is kept per browser under `ai-rover:scene-quality`, and `useSceneQuality()` is the one place a settings control writes it. Measured on the playground stop scene (mars fixture, 1920×1080 canvas, RTX 3080 through ANGLE/Vulkan, headless Chromium, GPU busy share and board power from `nvidia-smi`): per-frame cost was never the problem, about 0.7 ms of GPU per frame at a mid clock, and each content knob moved it by less than the run-to-run noise except the rover's shadow and the pixel ratio; what loaded the GPU was redrawing an unchanged frame at every display refresh.

Shadow map size, PCF radius (three's PCF takes five taps whatever the radius), the sky dome and the tone mapping showed no measurable saving, so the sky and the blur radius are no knobs; the map size stays one for the memory a 2048² depth map holds on small GPUs.

- **Everything on the map is inspectable.** Stops, ghosts, submissions, the drive's destination flag (its author, distance and bearing from the stop it left, planned path and time, planned arrival) and the rover are hoverable on both views (a card beside the pointer: what it is, when, distance, outcome or judgment) and clickable to focus: the view eases onto it over 500 ms and a detail panel opens (floating on desktop, a sheet on phones; closing it clears focus, as does Escape). With a mouse, hover shows and click focuses; on touch, the first tap shows and the second focuses. 2D hit-testing uses a 12 px tolerance with priority rover > destination > submission > death > stop; 3D picks invisible volumes around the markers, one raycast per frame. The recentre control returns focus to the rover, follows it while it drives in 2D until the user pans, and releases any focused ghost. The public state carries what the cards need (which segment reached a stop and when; a death's segment, reasons, time and distance; the driving segment's author and planned route figures, never its outcome).
- **3D is lazy and optional.** The map has a 2D/3D toggle, 2D by default, the choice kept per browser; three.js loads as its own chunk (~213 KB gzip) only when 3D is chosen, adding ~9 KB gzip to the home page. Picking stays a 2D affordance. Journey pages live at `/drives` because `/journey/**` serves the blobs.
- **The rover is NASA/JPL's own model.** The 3D rover is built by `scripts/rover-model.ts` from two NASA/JPL sources of the same vehicle, each at a pinned commit: NASA's Perseverance glTF (`NASA-3D-Resources`) gives every visible part with its materials, textures and glass (the arm stowed as the rover drives, the antennas, the mast deployed at the frame of its animation where the head holds at the top), and JPL's `m2020-urdf-models` gives the joints. NASA's geometry is fitted onto the URDF by the six wheel centres (0.2 mm rms) and cut into the URDF's links: wheels by wheel centre, suspension pieces by the URDF mesh they lie on; the differential's linkage, which the URDF has no link for, is a crank on each rocker and a rod from its top to the bar's end, the rod kept pointing at the crank at run time (`extras.aim`), so the bar turns 0.428 rad per radian of rocker. The arm is five joint nodes (`arm_1` … `arm_5`, the URDF's `JOINT1_ENC` … `JOINT5_ENC`), at rest in the stowed pose from the rover's own telemetry (the joint resolvers in a sol-100 Navcam label), NASA's five arm pivots lying on the URDF's axes to within 4 mm; the turret and its instruments hang from `arm_5`. One node per URDF joint, with the joint, its axis, limits and baked value in `extras`; the URDF's frame (x forward, y right, z down) is converted to the scene's x forward, y left, z up, after which rocker, bogie, differential and wheel joints map one-to-one to the solver's angles. Glass is blended rather than transmitted (transmission costs a second pass of the whole scene per frame). Each moving part draws one call per atlas page it uses plus one for its glass (blending needs its own draw): NASA's 47 materials are baked into pages shared by parts cut from the same meshes (chassis, mast, suspension, wheels), textured materials by copying the texels their triangles cover texel for texel (islands on an 8-texel grid congruent with the source's, so nothing is resampled and the first mip levels match), factor-only ones as palette blocks, normal scales baked in; pages are at most 2048 texels a side, so the chassis takes three; the arm has its own pages. The specular and IOR extensions of three dielectric materials are dropped (a slightly stronger highlight) so no opaque page needs the physical shader. Colour pages are re-encoded lossy WebP within 1 % of NASA's decoded texels, normals near-lossless (lossy coding speckles smooth metal). Full resolution (199k triangles, 3.6 MB with meshopt and WebP). One model on every device, and exactly two builds of it: the full one and a low-poly one, `rover-ghost.glb`, 1 988 triangles and 49 KB on the full model's nodes, with the same joints, transforms and extras, so it articulates identically and one posing routine drives either. The low-poly model is loaded and cached once, geometry shared, and every copy is drawn in a look (`RoverLook`), each look one material shared by all its copies: `ghost`, the red translucent unlit tint of the death markers, casting no shadow (a red, translucent silhouette needs no detail); `standin`, an opaque lit standard material in the rover's off-white paint, slightly metallic, casting and receiving shadows, fogged. The rover draws the stand-in while the full model loads, posed by the same rig and joints (suspension, differential, wheel spin, steering, arm, night pose included), and crossfades to the full model once decoded, after which the low-poly copy stays as the full model's shadow caster, drawn with neither colour nor depth; past the quality tier's distance it is drawn in the stand-in look again in place of the full model, one decision from what has loaded and how far the camera is. If the full model fails the stand-in stays, and casts; if the low-poly model fails, nothing is drawn for the rover until the full model is in, the scene saying the rover model is unavailable (the route, trail and markers drawn as ever), and the full model then casts its own shadow and is drawn at any distance. It is a primitive proxy, one model for every use, small enough to load first: each part a few convex primitives fitted to its own geometry in its node's frame, each a closed solid wound outward with flat normals. Each wheel is a tube of its true diameter, width and rim with its hub (spokes left out). Every other part's triangles fall into connected pieces (a piece whose halves fill much less than it split along its principal axes), grouped pairwise into a tree by the least empty volume a pair adds; each piece is offered its least-volume box, prisms of its convex outline along its principal axes and its node's, and least-squares cylinders about them, kept by volume overlap with the piece per triangle; the fit starts from the pieces up to 25 cm, then merges pieces into their parent's primitive, drops small ones or takes cheaper primitives, whichever loses least per triangle saved, until the budget holds, the loss being the growth of where the silhouettes from six orthographic views (top, front, side, three obliques) and the volume on 1.5 cm cubes differ from the full model's, plus a small cost per primitive so pieces merge where that costs little. Primitives reaching more than 1 cm past their part's extent are cut back to it, and no change may leave a part more than 1 cm short of it. It fits in about 12 s. The model's tests hold it to an IoU of 0.85 or more from each view (0.87 to 0.94), each part to within 2 cm of its full extent, every part closed with no triangle under 1 mm², and flat normals. Ghosts gain the full model only while focused, so at most two full models exist at once: the rover and the focused ghost. The swap waits for the full model to be decoded and crossfades from the silhouette, the same 150 ms crossfade as the rover's stand-in (both models drawn through transient transparent copies of their materials, so the shared ones never fade), so nothing flickers; unfocusing returns to the cached low-poly copy instantly. Credit NASA/JPL-Caltech (`public/models/rover/CREDITS.md`); no implied endorsement.
- **The survey is a circle.** Chunks are generated to the stop radius plus a margin so the circle is fully inside them; nothing beyond the circle is revealed, planned over or pickable, and both views draw it as background (the page in 2D, the sky in 3D) behind a thin ring (in 3D it casts no shadow either), so fog keeps meaning unexplored and reachable.
- **Fog hides, on both views.** Unrevealed ground is not drawn. The 2D map fills it with an opaque seeded-noise fog (light or dark with the colour mode) whose 2-cell soft edge lies on the revealed side; contours stop at the fog. The 3D scene draws fogged vertices at a smoothed height (mean of revealed ground within a 24 m window, else the stop's revealed mean) eased from adjacent revealed vertices so the frontier has no cliff, flat under the light, in the scene's fog colour (see the sky entry below); routes drape on the drawn surface, never the true one. Reveals fade in over 600 ms, repainting only the touched rectangles at most ten times a second. The chunks still carry the true data (scripting can read it; accepted), but nobody sees it by looking.
- **Ground has three visual states.** Directly in the rover's line of sight now: full height tint and shade. Seen earlier but not in sight now: geometry and contours without colour (desaturated), so the map and the scene show what is known without pretending it is being watched. Never seen: fog. The current line of sight is computed in the browser from the rover's position over the revealed ground, at most every 500 ms while the rover moves, and once per page with the fog and its fade: the scene and the floating 2D map draw the same result. Everything the computation touches beyond the revealed ground stays invisible.
- **Terrain arrives as one pack per stop.** Besides the per-chunk blobs, a stop publishes a single deflated pack of all its chunks; the browser streams it and paints chunks as they decode, falling back to per-chunk fetches (twelve in flight) when a pack is missing. After publishing a stop the server requests its manifest, mask and pack once through the site's public address so the CDN's durable cache is warm before the first visitor; nothing of an in-progress segment is ever primed.
- **The 2D map reads as relief.** A five-stop height tint over the disk's height range, hillshade with 2.5× vertical exaggeration (shared with the 3D terrain normals), 1 m contours with every fifth line heavier, drawn only on revealed ground and fading in past 1 px/m zoom, plus a legend with the height range and interval.
- **Replaying the journey.** Consecutive settled segments play back to back over one clock, skipping the idle time between them; the disk, judgment and instruments switch at each stop, the next segment's slices and disk are prefetched at 80 %, and `n`/`p` jump between segments. The list page offers "since last visit" (a per-browser mark that only moves forward), the last 24 hours, the last 7 days and a segment-number range; the segments API takes `from`/`to` and `since`, settled drives only.
- **Day and night follow the sol clock.** The stop scene's sun stands where it would at Jezero (18.4° N) at the sol time shown: now on the live map, the moment of the drive in a replay. The season is fixed at the northern spring equinox, since the mission has no calendar, so the sun rises due east at a quarter of the sol and sets due west at three quarters. Night stays dim but readable: exposure makes up 70 % of a fall in light on a log scale and never rises past 8× the high sun's, so the ground stays readable after dusk and the turret lamp still shows. The look is contrast 1.35 at saturation 1.0: Blender's "Punchy" contrast without its saturation boost, which turns the relief tints from rust to crimson.
- **In 3D the sky owns the fog colour.** Distance haze, the sky background and unseen ground all take the sky's horizon colour at the sun's elevation, whatever the page's colour mode, so what is too far to make out and what has not been seen look alike at every hour. The 2D map keeps its light or dark fog fill with the colour mode.
- **3D is the goal, the stop disk is the first step.** The full free-roam streaming world is the destination; the first implementation renders the current stop disk with chunk LOD, the rover playing keyframes, a follow camera, trail and ghosts, and confirms the project working locally in a production-like posture.

## Layout

- **Full viewport, HUD on top.** The scene fills the viewport on every device with a slim top bar (2D/3D switch, playback status, instruments toggle, links). Panels are floating windows at 768 px and wider (drag, resize, minimise, close, snapping, layout kept per browser under a versioned key) and bottom sheets that leave the scene touchable below that. 3D is the default view, remembered per browser. The HUD is on by default on desktop and off on phones; keys `1`/`2` switch views, `h` toggles the HUD, `space` pauses (holding sim time in replay), `l` goes live. Only the base scene and the floating map redraw per frame; everything else updates at 10 Hz. Picking happens on the base 2D view; from 3D a button switches there and the view returns after a submission.
- **The 2D/3D switch loads three.js on the home page by default** (about 232 KB gzip, lazily): the earlier "3D is lazy and optional" default of 2D is superseded.
- **Naming.** Approvals are "LGTM"; the idle wait after the first submission is the "planning phase". The whole journey is the **mission** (the schema, API paths and blob keys say so); one drive from a stop to the next is a **segment** ("Segment N") in the interface and in the code alike. "Mission" is never used for a single drive.

## Dashboard and instruments

- **Instrument conventions.** Sol = 88,775.244 s with the mission epoch at 00:00 local mean solar time of sol 0 (the world has no longitude). AutoNav-style efficiency = ground distance ÷ elapsed sim time ÷ 4.2 cm/s. Commanded distance = mean middle-wheel spin × wheel radius. Slip is measured over the last metre, warning above 30 % and red at the 60 % stuck threshold. Revealed area = revealed vertices × cell area. Unseen stretches of a planned route are drawn flat and dotted so hidden terrain never leaks through a slope profile. Chart colours are `--viz-*` tokens in `main.css` for light and dark.
- **Every metric gets its own instrument, built in isolation.** Each reading the record exposes (attitude and suspension angles, speed and odometer, mission clock, slip, reveals, planner telemetry, round countdown, per-user tallies) is developed as a standalone component with its own tailored graphic: an animated side and rear view of the rocker-bogie showing the live rocker, bogie, pitch and roll rather than a dial; charts for series. The complete dashboard assembles them last.
- **Vote cards show the judgment.** Distance, planned path and detour, bearing, estimated time and arrival (wall-clock and sol), seen-ground slopes, turns, unseen share, loose ground, and Jev's feasibility, risk and both confidences, with like count and submitter.
- **AutoNav-style efficiency** (effective versus commanded speed per segment) is a public metric alongside the planner telemetry.

## Code layout and data formats

- **Terrain code is shared, pure TypeScript.** Height function, masks, planner and viewshed live under `shared/utils/terrain/` (the folder Nuxt auto-imports) with no Node or browser API, so the server computes disks with exactly the code the browser's preview planner runs over fetched chunks.
- **Chunks are binary and deflated.** Each chunk is one blob: a small fixed header, `Float32` heights, `Uint8` masks, deflated at write time with the web-standard `CompressionStream` and inflated natively in the browser. No parse cost, compact storage.
- **The browser loads progressively.** The chunks under the pick ring and the viewport come first so the page shows ground immediately; the rest of the disk and any replay data load lazily, since a visitor checking in mid-segment may never replay it.
- **Development-only code lives in one local module.** Routes, pages, playground and fixtures that exist only for development are registered by the `dev` module after its `nuxt.options.dev` check, so no production or test build bundles them; nothing dev-only sits under `server/` or `app/pages/`. Files still carry a `TODO(dev-only)` comment when they are throwaway rather than permanent tooling.
- **Instruments are built in an in-house playground.** The dev module serves one page per instrument and per scene, fed by fixture data from a real drive record with a time scrubber (a page needing no fixture runs on its own clock: the rover's joints, and its scripted motions, a point turn, an arc and the arm at dusk and dawn, steering and turning on the same S-curves as the arm), following the page-per-component idea of component playgrounds; Compodium is tried but not relied on under Nuxt 5 and TypeScript 7.
- **Scale parameters are world configuration, not constants.** Chunk size (64 m), cell size (1 m), mast height (2 m), slope limit (16°) and crater statistics are fields of the world definition, so they can be tuned without touching the algorithms.

## Failure vote, moderation, community

- **"Rover not moving" flags with a quorum that scales to the room.** While a drive plays, signed-in viewers may flag the rover as stuck. When the flags of distinct users within 10 minutes reach the quorum and the released playback shows under 0.5 m of straight-line progress over that window, the drive's tail is rewritten: the next unreleased slice becomes the rover standing still with a `stuck` event and the failure, later slices are deleted, and the drive ends at that slice's release, so the live stream and replays end cleanly and nothing changes before release. The quorum is `min(5, max(2, ceil(active / 2)))` where `active` is the number of distinct users who submitted, LGTMed or flagged in the round. An automatic backstop cuts a drive that shows no progress for 15 minutes; it is computed once when the drive is produced, since reads tick only when something is due. Thresholds live in mission rules; failure reasons are `flagged-not-moving` and `no-progress`.
- **Operations without a terminal.** The first mission is landed from `/admin`: a form protected by `NUXT_ADMIN_TOKEN` (compared in constant time) that creates stop 0 and the opening round with the default world and rules and publishes its disk; it refuses when a mission already exists and answers 404 when no token is configured. Everything else an operator needs (variables, repository link) lives in the hosting UI.
- **Moderation is a pause.** There is no free text to moderate. An operator pauses a mission with a message and their identity recorded (`mission_pause`, at most one active per mission); the dashboard shows the banner, and submissions and LGTMs answer 423 with the operator's message while it lasts. Set through the database.
- **Community page.** Per-user tallies (submissions won, LGTMs received, distance driven on your plans, failures) at `/community`, computed from settled segments, no extra tables.

## Open questions

- **Keyframe coordinates are absolute float32 world metres:** about 1 mm precision at 8 km from the origin, worse beyond; origin-relative frames become necessary if a mission travels far.
- **Per-metre `pause` events:** they double the event JSON while keyframes with zero speed already show the pauses; drop or keep.
- **Chunk payload:** deflate only reaches 0.70 on float32 heights (a 500 m disk is 3.3 MB deflated), so the 16-bit centimetre height encoding is now the likely next step before the map ships.
- **Biome design:** which biomes, how they are generated (noise-selected regions blending parameter sets), what the rover's analysis reports and how Jev uses it.

- **Vote updates:** like counts ride the same polled mission-state endpoint (short CDN TTL with stale-while-revalidate, purged by tag on each like) or get their own endpoint with a shorter TTL. The reference site checked for a Netlify WebSocket pattern turned out to run on Vercel with a separate PartyKit worker on Cloudflare for its live counters which is the split-worker option, not a Netlify-native one.
- **Terrain scale defaults:** the 64 m / 1 m / 16° defaults are starting points and the default relief is benign (almost everything traversable, see `research/measurements.md`). `research/rover-geometry-mars-terrain.md` recommends, from MOLA and HiRISE statistics: ~10 m RMS relief at 1 km with Hurst exponent 0.7 (octave gain ~0.62), ~250 craters/km² with a D^-2.8 cumulative size law, shallower degraded bowls (depth/diameter 0.05–0.1, 0.2 only when fresh), plus a rock population (cumulative fractional area k ≈ 0.07–0.10) the model does not have yet. Apply once the planner exists and blocked ground can be judged.
- **Failure rules:** auto-detection thresholds, exclusion radius around a failed spot, "not moving" community vote fallback.
